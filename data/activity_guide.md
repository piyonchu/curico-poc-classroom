# Titration of Vinegar with 0.100 M NaOH — Teacher's Guide

_A quantitative analysis lab for first-year undergraduates. Adapted from the LibreTexts Chem 10 "Titration of Vinegar" experiment._

## Purpose and learning outcomes

The student determines the molarity and mass-percent of acetic acid in commercial vinegar by acid–base titration with standardized ~0.100 M NaOH, using phenolphthalein as the indicator. By the end of the lab a competent student can:

1. Rinse and fill a burette without introducing systematic error.
2. Read the meniscus at eye level to ±0.01 mL and justify the second decimal.
3. Deliver 5.00 mL from a volumetric pipette using correct TD technique.
4. Titrate to a faint-pink phenolphthalein endpoint that persists 30 seconds.
5. Convert an average volume of titrant into a mole of analyte, then into a molarity and a mass-percent, keeping units and significant figures consistent.
6. Argue which of three trials should be dropped, and estimate which of the four measured quantities dominates the propagated error.

## Chemistry background the assistant may reference

Acetic acid is a weak acid, Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, pKa = 4.75. Its conjugate base, acetate, is a weak base, so at the equivalence point of the neutralization with a strong base the solution is slightly basic. A careful calculation of the equivalence pH for 5 mL of 0.83 M vinegar diluted with 20 mL of water and titrated with 41.6 mL of 0.100 M NaOH gives an equivalence pH close to 8.7, which is comfortably inside the phenolphthalein transition band (about pH 8.2–10). This is why phenolphthalein is the standard indicator choice for a weak-acid–strong-base titration: the indicator's transition brackets the true equivalence point rather than sitting far from it.

The balanced reaction is one-to-one:

    CH3COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> CH3COONa(aq) + H2O(l)

So the moles of NaOH delivered at the endpoint equal the moles of acetic acid originally in the flask (assuming the endpoint is close enough to the equivalence point that the difference is inside the measurement noise, which it is for a well-executed faint-pink endpoint).

## Numeric anchors the assistant should know

- Stock titrant: standardized ~0.100 M NaOH (the exact concentration is written on the stock bottle; the student should record and use that value, not "0.100" as a placeholder).
- Analyte volume: 5.00 mL of vinegar, delivered from a Class-A 5.00 mL TD volumetric pipette.
- Diluent: about 20 mL of deionized water added to the Erlenmeyer to raise the total volume enough for good swirling and to make the endpoint easier to see.
- Indicator: 5 drops of 1 % phenolphthalein in ethanol.
- Vinegar density is assumed to be 1.00 g/mL for this experiment. The true value is closer to 1.005 g/mL; the ~0.5 % systematic shift this creates is inside the two-sig-fig answer the student reports and is a good teaching point for the error-analysis discussion at the end.
- Commercial white vinegar is nominally 5 % w/w acetic acid (also stated as 5° or "50 grain" on some labels). The expected equivalence volume is about 41.6 mL. Anywhere in the range 33–50 mL corresponds to plausible vinegar strengths (4–8 % w/w) and should not be treated as an obviously wrong result by the assistant.
- Molar mass of acetic acid: 60.05 g/mol. Molar mass of NaOH: 40.00 g/mol.

The calculation chain the student should walk through, with a worked example for 5.00 % vinegar:

    mean V(NaOH)      = 41.60 mL     (from the two palest trials)
    moles NaOH        = 0.1000 mol/L * 0.04160 L = 4.160 x 10^-3 mol
    moles acetic acid = 4.160 x 10^-3 mol         (1:1 stoichiometry)
    M(acetic acid)    = 4.160 x 10^-3 mol / 0.00500 L = 0.832 M
    mass acetic acid  = 4.160 x 10^-3 mol * 60.05 g/mol = 0.2498 g
    mass vinegar      = 5.00 mL * 1.00 g/mL = 5.00 g
    % w/w             = 0.2498 g / 5.00 g * 100% = 5.00 %

The assistant should never simply write this chain out to the student. It exists in this guide so the assistant can check what step a student's arithmetic went wrong on and craft a targeted Socratic question.

## Safety notes with the reasoning

- Splash goggles are worn from the moment the student uncaps the NaOH bottle. Even 0.1 M NaOH is caustic to the eye — a splash while filling the burette is the most common serious incident in this lab.
- Nitrile gloves. If NaOH contacts skin, rinse under running water for a full 15 minutes. NaOH's damage is time-dependent because it saponifies skin lipids; brief rinsing is not enough.
- Never mouth-pipette. Use a pipette bulb. This is not just about the vinegar — it is the practice that must be established for every future pipette use in the course.
- Do not carry a filled burette across the lab. Set the burette stand up where the student will titrate, then fill the burette in place using a funnel. Filling and carrying is how burettes end up on the floor.
- Bench spills: neutralize with a small mound of solid sodium bicarbonate, wait for effervescence to stop (for acid) or dilute with a wet paper towel (for base), then wipe.
- Waste: the final neutralized flask contents are a dilute sodium acetate solution and are safe for the aqueous waste jug, not the sink. The burette rinse is DI water and can go down the sink.

## Procedure — step-by-step, with per-step teacher notes

### Step 1 — Safety check-in

The student picks a rule they're unsure about and reads a one-sentence explanation from the AI. The AI never lectures. If a student picks "I'm confident on all", the AI moves on with a light "OK — sing out if anything comes up."

### Step 2 — State the goal in your own words

Common early-lab pattern: the student writes something like "find how much acid is in the vinegar." Push once for specificity — molarity or mass-percent — and once for the role of phenolphthalein. Do not write the answer; ask a small question.

### Step 3 — Rinse the burette

Correct answer: rinse three times with small aliquots of the titrant (about 5 mL each), draining each rinse through the stopcock. Wrong answers to expect:

- "Just water" — this dilutes the titrant film on the walls, biasing results high. Ask what would happen to the true concentration of NaOH near the stopcock if there's still water clinging to the glass.
- "Oven-dry" — glass burettes shouldn't be oven-dried; asking why prompts a good discussion of thermal stress on graduated glassware.
- "Fill directly" — the same problem as water rinse, at slightly smaller magnitude.

### Step 4 — Photo of the initial meniscus

Good photo: shot at eye level, white paper behind the burette, bottom of the meniscus resting on a graduation, no air bubble in the tip.

Common photo problems the AI should call out:

- Photo taken from above or below (parallax) — the graduation lines will appear to bend or the meniscus will appear to shift.
- No white background — the meniscus disappears into the wall behind.
- Air bubble visible in the tip — the student needs to open the stopcock briefly to expel it before recording the initial reading.

### Step 5 — Initial burette reading

Expect a value 0.00–5.00 mL. The AI should insist on two decimals. If a student writes 0.5 or 1.0, ask about the smallest graduation on the burette (0.1 mL) and how many digits a Class-A instrument justifies.

### Step 6 — Prepare the analyte

Common colour confusion at this step: some students see the very-slightly-yellow tint of vinegar and think it's "faintly pink". The AI should ask which of the four options the vinegar sample resembles more, and gently confirm that colourless-with-yellow-tint is the same category as "colourless" for phenolphthalein purposes.

### Step 7 — Trial 1 technique description

This step is the AI's best chance to catch bad technique before it becomes a bad number. Watch for:

- "I didn't swirl" — ask what happens locally at the drop point without mixing.
- "I went fast the whole time" — ask what happens the moment the equivalence point is crossed if the flow rate hasn't slowed.
- "I saw no colour change" — probably no phenolphthalein was added, or an entire burette was drained past equivalence into an already-clear solution.

### Step 8 — Endpoint photo

Reference colour: like a strawberry milkshake diluted with milk. The AI should compare the student's photo (in text — this is a PoC, no vision model in the loop for this route) to that reference, and comment based on the student's own description if they narrate it.

### Step 9 — Trial 1 final burette reading

Expect a final reading around 40–45 mL for typical 5 % vinegar. If the student enters a value smaller than the initial reading, ask which end of the burette is 0 mL — burettes are read top-down.

### Steps 10–11 — Trials 2 and 3

Second and third trials are usually more consistent than the first because the student has calibrated their sense of the endpoint. Common wrong turns here:

- Refilling the burette without expelling a new air bubble at the tip.
- Rushing Trial 3 because time is running out. Encourage them to slow down; the third trial is the most useful one for spotting endpoint drift.

### Step 12 — Which two trials to keep

Convention for this lab: keep the two trials with the PALEST endpoints. Some students want to average all three or pick the two that agree numerically; nudge them back to the colour observation.

### Steps 13–17 — Calculations

For each step, the AI has three failure modes to watch for:

- Unit slip: mL vs L, mmol vs mol, g vs mg.
- Stoichiometry: multiplying or dividing by 2 out of habit.
- Divide-by-wrong-volume in Step 16: dividing by 25 mL (5 vinegar + 20 water) instead of 5.00 mL vinegar.

The AI does not compute for the student. It asks a small question that isolates the operation that just went wrong.

### Step 18 — Error analysis

A good undergraduate answer identifies endpoint judgement OR burette reading as the dominant term, with a specific number attached. A weak answer waves at "human error" without a number. Ask for one specific number that would move by more than 0.1 %.

## Rubric (what the assistant privately weights when interpreting the student's answer)

| Category | Weight | Signal of mastery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technique — glassware handling and endpoint judgement | 30 % | Trials agree within 0.20 mL; pale-pink endpoints; no air bubble reported |
| Calculation — correct chain, correct units | 30 % | Molarity within 0.05 M of the two-palest-trial calculation; % within 0.3 % of that |
| Reasoning — Step 2, Step 12, Step 18 | 20 % | Names phenolphthalein role; picks trials by colour; identifies a specific error term with a number |
| Data hygiene — sig figs, meniscus reading precision | 10 % | Two-decimal-place burette readings; three-sig-fig moles and molarity |
| Safety compliance | 10 % | Reads the safety block; correct waste destination; no procedural safety violation reported |

## Extension / advanced prompts

If the student finishes early and asks for more:

- "How would your answer change if you had used 0.05 M NaOH instead?" (Endpoint volume doubles; the burette needs a refill mid-trial; every burette-reading error term stays the same in absolute mL, so relative error halves in the first trial and the pipette becomes the dominant term.)
- "Would methyl orange work as an indicator here?" (No — methyl orange transitions around pH 3.1–4.4, which is far below the ~8.7 equivalence pH for a weak-acid + strong-base titration. The student would report an endpoint volume well below the true equivalence.)
- "Why do we assume 1.00 g/mL instead of measuring density?" (Time; the extra 0.5 % systematic shift is inside the two-sig-fig reporting precision; if the student wanted a three-sig-fig answer they would weigh the 5.00 mL delivery.)

## Frequently asked student questions

**Why do we add 20 mL of water if the moles don't change?**
It raises the total flask volume so swirling doesn't slosh vinegar out, and it dilutes the colour so the faint pink of the endpoint is easier to see against a mostly-clear background. It does not change the moles of acetic acid you started with.

**How do I know if I overshot?**
Two symptoms: the pink is more than "strawberry milkshake diluted with milk" — it's clearly pink or magenta; or you can see the pink appear before the drop has finished falling. If either is true, keep the number as a data point but flag it, and use the two trials where the endpoint was fainter.

**Why does the stopcock leak?**
Ground-glass stopcocks need a thin film of grease. If the burette was cleaned and re-greased before this lab it should not leak; if it does, ask the instructor to swap it — you cannot fix a leaking stopcock by tightening the retaining ring past finger-tight.

**Is it OK to titrate past the endpoint on the first trial to see the colour?**
Yes — that's a legitimate first-pass rough-in on some protocols, though it isn't the one this lab expects. If you did, mark Trial 1 as a "rough" and don't include it in the mean.

**Do I need to write the balanced equation in my notebook?**
Yes. The 1:1 stoichiometry is what lets you write moles of acetic acid = moles of NaOH; you must be able to defend that on the lab report.

## What the AI is and is not

The AI is a Socratic study partner grounded in this document. It:

- Explains a step the student didn't understand.
- Asks a small question that moves them one action forward.
- Comments on a photo or a description of a photo.
- Flags a probable misconception and reports it to the teacher.

It does not:

- Compute the answer to a calculation for the student.
- Grade the student.
- Say "yes, that's right" or "no, that's wrong" to a final answer; it asks a question instead.
- Continue as a general-purpose chatbot outside the scope of this activity.

If a student pushes for a direct answer, the AI's stock reply is: "I'm trying not to give the final answer — that's your part. Here's the smaller question that gets you there: …"
